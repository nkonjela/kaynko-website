How to Turn Any Android Phone Into a Cash Register in Minutes

Kaynko POS Team · 6 min read

A cash register used to mean a heavy machine on the counter, or a dedicated terminal with its own cables and contract. For a lot of small shops today, the simplest cash register is the Android phone already sitting behind the till. Here's what actually has to happen to turn that phone into a working point of sale.

What "becoming a cash register" actually requires

A cash register, at its core, needs to do four things: show what's for sale and its price, add items up, take a payment, and keep a record of what happened. A modern point-of-sale app does all four, plus the things an old-fashioned till never could — tracking stock as it sells, remembering customers who buy on credit, and producing a report at the end of the day.

Setting it up

▶

Demo clip: install → add a product → first sale (placeholder — full walkthrough coming to the Kaynko YouTube channel)

  1. Install the app. Kaynko POS installs from Google Play like any other app — no separate hardware kit to order or wait for.
  2. Add your business details. Business name, currency, and a few basic settings.
  3. Add your products. Name, price, and (optionally) a stock quantity and barcode. A handful of products is enough to start selling the same day; more can be added any time, including in bulk from a spreadsheet.
  4. Open a cash session. Count and enter your opening float, and the till is live.
  5. Sell. Search or scan a product, take payment, and the sale is recorded.

None of this needs a technician, a setup call, or a manual. It's built to be usable by someone running a shop for the first time, not just someone comfortable with software.

What the phone replaces

A phone running a proper POS app can replace several separate things a shop might otherwise juggle: a physical till for cash, a paper ledger or notebook for stock and customer credit, and a calculator for totals and change. It can also connect to the hardware a slightly bigger shop wants without needing new, proprietary equipment — a Bluetooth thermal printer for receipts, or a Bluetooth barcode scanner for higher item volumes, both of which pair with the phone directly rather than requiring a specific brand of terminal.

What it doesn't require

It's worth being specific about what a phone-based till genuinely doesn't need, since this is often the actual hesitation: it doesn't need a permanent internet connection to make a sale (see our guide to offline-first POS apps), it doesn't need dedicated card-payment hardware to accept cash, mobile money, bank transfer, or credit sales, and it doesn't need a second device for the owner — the same reports a cashier's phone produces can be checked from any device the owner logs into.

When a phone genuinely isn't enough

To be fair about it: a single phone has one screen and one set of hands behind it. A shop that needs several checkout points running at once, or a team large enough that one phone can't realistically be passed around, will want more than one device — which is why multi-cashier support (separate staff logins on the same or different devices) exists rather than forcing everyone through one phone. That's a team-management question, not a reason to rule out a phone-based till for a single counter.
