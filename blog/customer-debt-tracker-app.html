Tracking Customer Debt and IOUs in a Retail Shop: A Practical Guide

Kaynko POS Team · 7 min read

Selling on credit to regular customers is normal in a lot of retail shops — a trusted customer takes goods now and pays later, in full or over a few visits. The trouble isn't the practice itself, it's how it's usually tracked: a notebook that can be misread, misplaced, or simply forgotten about until a balance has grown far larger than anyone realized.

What a notebook actually gets wrong

A paper ledger has no way to flag a customer who's already over a comfortable limit, no running total that updates itself as partial payments come in, and no record that survives a torn page or a bad week. It also puts the whole system in one physical place — if the notebook is lost, so is the entire record of who owes what.

What a proper debt tracker does instead

  • A running balance per customer, updated automatically every time a credit sale or a payment is recorded — no manual addition required.
  • A credit limit, if you want one. A customer can be capped at a maximum balance; a sale that would push them over it is blocked before it happens, rather than discovered after the fact.
  • Partial payments against a balance, not just all-or-nothing settlement — a customer can pay down what they owe over several visits, and the balance reflects it each time.
  • A record that isn't tied to one physical object. Customer and debt data lives in the app's own database, and can be backed up automatically.

How it works in practice

  1. Register a customer once — name and contact details, and a credit limit if you want to set one.
  2. At checkout, choose "credit" as the payment method for that sale — this requires a registered customer, so a credit sale can never be anonymous.
  3. The sale adds to that customer's running balance.
  4. When they pay — in full or in part — record the payment against their balance from the customer's record.
  5. If a payment is recorded in error, it can be reversed by a manager, rather than simply deleted and lost from the record.

A trust problem this solves without anyone noticing

The real risk with notebook-based credit tracking usually isn't bad intentions — it's just that paper is fragile. A phone falling in water, a notebook left in a bag that gets rained on, or simply misplacing it for a week during a busy season can genuinely mean losing track of real money owed to the shop. The same risk exists for digital records kept only on one device: if that phone is lost, stolen, or broken, the data goes with it unless it's backed up somewhere else.

The bigger picture

Customer credit is a normal, often necessary part of running a shop that serves regular customers. It doesn't need to be tracked on faith and good memory — a system that updates a balance automatically, enforces a limit if you want one, and survives a lost phone turns an informal arrangement into something a shop owner can actually rely on.
